thinking with type

thinking with type

Ellen Lupton, 2010

Letter

This is not a book about fonts. This is not a book about fonts. It is a book about how to use them. Typefaces are an essential resource employed by graphic designers, just as glass, stone, steel, and countless other materials are employed by architects. Graphic designers sometimes create their own fonts and custom lettering. More commonly, however, they tap the vast library of existing typefaces, choosing and combining them in response to a particular audience or situation. To do this with wit and wisdom requires knowledge of how—and why—letterforms have evolved.

Words originated as gestures of the body. The first typefaces were directly modeled on the forms of calligraphy.

Typefaces, however, are not bodily gestures—they are manufactured images designed for infinite repetition.

The history of typography reflects a continual tension between the hand and the machine, the organic and the geometric, the human body and the abstract system. These tensions, which marked the birth of printed letters over five hundred year ago, continue to energize typography today.

I found this point especially interesting because it made me think about type as image. We usually think of letters as something we read, but they are also visual forms that we see and recognize.

One example that came to my mind is the CNN logo. Instead of treating C, N, and N as three completely separate letters, they are connected by one continuous line. This connection makes the three letters feel like one complete shape, and I think this is part of what makes the logo so recognizable. Even though we are still reading the letters “CNN,” we are also recognizing the overall shape of the wordmark.

This made me think about how we sometimes recognize familiar words or logos without reading every letter one by one. A small typographic decision, such as changing the spacing, connecting letters, or adjusting part of a letterform, can change the way we experience a word and give it its own identity. To me, typography can work as both language and image at the same time.

Type as program & narrative

Responding in 1967 to the rise of electronic communication, the Dutch designer Wim Crouwel published designs for a “new alphabet” constructed from straight lines. Rejecting centuries of typographic convention, he designed his letters for optimal display on a video screen (CRT), where curves and angles are rendered with horizontal scan lines. In a brochure promoting his new alphabet, subtitled “An Introduction for a Programmed Typography,” he proposed a design methodology in which decisions are rule-based and systematic.

In the early 1990s, as digital design tools began supporting the seamless reproduction and integration of media, many designers grew dissatisfied with clean, unsullied surfaces, seeking instead to plunge the letter into the harsh and caustic world of physical processes. Letters, which for centuries had sought perfection in ever more exact technologies, became scratched, bent, bruised, and polluted.

Although the 1990s are best remembered for images of decay, typeface designers continued to build a repertoire of general purpose fonts designed to comfortably accommodate broad bodies of text. Rather than narrate the story of their own birth, such workhorse fonts provide graphic designers with flexible palettes of letterforms coordinated within larger families.

In 2000 Tobias Frere-Jones introduced Gotham, derived from letters found at the Port Authority Bus Terminal in New York City. Gotham expresses a no-nonsense, utilitarian attitude that persists today alongside the aesthetics of grunge, neofuturism, pop-culture parodies, and straight historical revivals that are all part of contemporary typography.

When choosing a font, graphic designers consider the history of typefaces and their current connotations as well as their formal qualities.

The goal is to find an appropriate match between a style of letters and the specific social situation and body of content that define the project at hand. There is no playbook that assigns a fixed meaning or function to every typeface; each designer must confront the library of possibilities in light of a project's unique circumstances.

I believe different typefaces carry different meanings and feelings. There are not necessarily bad typefaces, but there can be bad decisions—it is more about whether the choice is appropriate for the content. I also think the feeling of a typeface can come from its history. The way a typeface looks is often connected to the time and technology in which it was created.

For example, for an essay or newspaper, we may prefer Humanist or Garalde typefaces because they can give the text a more traditional and formal feeling. For fashion magazines, especially publications such as Vogue, Didone typefaces are often used for titles because their strong contrast between thick and thin strokes creates a more elegant and sophisticated feeling. On the other hand, technology or gaming brands often use sans serif or geometric typefaces, which can communicate a sense of precision and the beauty of mathematical forms. And these associations are not only created by the shapes themselves, but also by the history behind them. Geometric typefaces, for example, became closely connected with modernism and a period when designers were moving away from traditional forms and starting to look toward a more mechanical and modern visual language.

Of course, these choices should still be made with legibility and readability in mind. What I find interesting is that choosing a typeface can be similar to choosing the cover of a book. Sometimes, the first impression is already enough to communicate something about the content before we actually start reading. At the same time, I think these impressions come from both the visual form of a typeface and what we have learned to associate with it over time. Just as Lupton states, “There is no playbook that assigns a fixed meaning or function to every typeface.” To me, this means that understanding the history and characteristics of a typeface can help us make a decision, but it does not mean there is only one correct way to use it.

Type Classification

Type classification A basic system for classifying typefaces was devised in the nineteenth century, when printers sought to identify a heritage for their own craft analogous to that of art history. Humanist letterforms are closely connected to calligraphy and the movement of the hand. Transitional and modern typefaces are more abstract and less organic. These three main groups correspond roughly to the Renaissance, Baroque, and Enlightenment periods in art and literature.

Historians and critics of typography have since proposed more finely grained schemes that attempt to better capture the diversity of letterforms.

Designers in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries have continued to create new typefaces based on historic characteristics.

I used to think about type classification mainly as a way to identify and organize typefaces based on their visual characteristics. After reading about the different classifications, what I found interesting is that these differences in form are not random. They can reflect different ways of creating letterforms, from handwriting to more abstract and geometric forms.

This also connects to Lupton's earlier statement that “The history of typography reflects a continual tension between the hand and the machine, the organic and the geometric, the human body and the abstract system.” I think this contrast becomes easier to notice when comparing different typefaces. When I choose a typeface, one letter I usually pay attention to is the letter “O.” In a Humanist typeface, it can feel more organic, while in a geometric typeface, it can appear almost like a perfect circle. Small differences like this can affect not only how individual letters look, but also how they work together within a paragraph.

In a well-designed paragraph, I believe no single letter should stand out too much unless there is a reason for it. Choosing a typeface is not only about how each letter looks individually, but also about the visual rhythm they create together.

Understanding type classification gives me another way to look at those details. It helps me identify the characteristics of a typeface more quickly and understand where some of those characteristics come from. Instead of only asking whether a typeface looks right for a project, I can also think about what makes it look and feel that way, which can help me make more thoughtful design decisions.

Typeface on screen

During the early years of the World Wide Web, designers were forced to work within the narrow range of typefaces commonly installed on the computers of their end users.

Since then, several techniques have emerged for embedding fonts within web content or for delivering fonts to end users when they visit a site. In one approach, specially formatted fonts are hosted on a third-party server and then downloaded by users; designers pay a fee for the service. Another approach implements the @font-face rule in CSS, which can download any kind of digital font hosted on a server; only typefaces licensed for this use can be accessed legally via @font-face.

What I found interesting about this section is how much technology has changed the possibilities of typography on screen. In the early web, designers had to work with a limited range of typefaces, while today we have much more freedom in how type can be used online. But I think designing typography for digital formats is not only about having access to more typefaces.

On a printed page, typography usually exists within a fixed space. On a website, however, type needs to work across different screen sizes, but it can also move, change, or respond to the way people interact with it. This made me think about how technology does not only remove limitations; it can also change what typography is able to do.

This reminded me of a quote from Erik van Blokland and Just van Rossum in one of the chapters: “Typography is now produced with sophisticated equipment that doesn't impose such rules. The only limitations are in our expectations.” The last sentence especially stood out to me. As someone who is more familiar with graphic design for print, I usually think about typography as something I arrange within a fixed space. Sometimes, I may continue to treat digital typography like a static printed page simply because that is what I am used to.

Designing for a screen makes me think differently because that space can constantly change. Instead of only considering where type is placed, I can also think deeply about how it behaves and how people interact with it.
